Ten-week plan

Check sources, compare two tools, and start building in week 5. Four meetings before the showcase give students time to test, fix, and watch someone try their tool.

10 in-person meetings · 25 hours of planned activities

Why ten weeks is recommended

A student can see a model produce an unsupported answer, learn how to check it, and bring that habit into their own project. Separate meetings for debugging and first-time use give them time to act on what they find.

Each three-hour meeting includes 150 minutes of planned activities and 30 minutes for breaks and questions. The original proposal was for 1.5 credits. The program would set credits for the chosen format.

Week 01 Start with the AI we already useConnect the tools students know to the companies, models, and tasks behind them.

What you’ll leave with

A labeled example of a company, model, and product, plus one question about an AI claim in healthcare.

Before class 15 min

Fill out the opening survey. Include personal and work or school examples, familiar companies, and words you would like someone to explain.

After class 20 min

Find one AI feature in a tool you already use. Record who makes it, the task it helps with, and one thing you would check.

Opening surveyEight questions about how students use AI and what they want to learn

Send 3–5 days before the first meeting. Ask for everyday examples without patient information, employer-confidential material, or private conversations. Summarize responses without names; access needs stay private.

  1. Where have you used AI in your personal life?

    List every use you can remember: the task, the tool if known, and whether it helped. Recipes, travel, shopping, writing, learning, images, and building things all count. ‘None yet’ and ‘not sure’ are valid answers.

  2. Where have you used AI at work or in your studies?

    Include tools you chose and features already inside another product: meeting summaries, email drafts, research, spreadsheets, coding, or a specific AI feature in an EHR such as Epic. Name the feature if you know it; using an EHR does not necessarily mean using AI.

  3. Which companies and products do you recognize?

    For each, select: not familiar / heard of it / tried it / use regularly. Include OpenAI (ChatGPT, Codex), Anthropic (Claude), Google (Gemini), Microsoft (Copilot), Epic, Abridge, Cohere Health, IQVIA, Ollama, and Lovable, plus an open ‘something else’ field.

  4. Which AI capabilities have you encountered?

    Select any: summarize, draft, search, transcribe, generate images or audio, predict, analyze data, write code, automate steps, or take actions in another app. Add an example or select ‘not sure.’

  5. Which terms have you heard, and which could you explain?

    For AI, machine learning, generative AI, LLM, training, post-training, fine-tuning, inference, prompt, token, context window, hallucination, RAG, agent, automation, API, and vibe coding, select: new to me / heard it / could explain it in one sentence. Add any other buzzwords you want unpacked.

  6. How confident do you feel today?

    Rate 1–5, from not yet confident to very confident: using AI personally; using it at work or school; explaining how it works; questioning a product claim; and building a tool with AI. Repeat these questions at the end of the course.

  7. What has surprised or frustrated you? What would you like to make?

    Describe one useful or disappointing experience, or a question you want answered. If you have a project idea, share it. You can change your mind later.

  8. What would help you participate?

    Share any device, account, access, or accessibility needs privately with the instructor. This helps the instructor prepare. You do not need to buy anything for the survey.

  2. 30 min

Case discussion Abridge / Hawaii Pacific Health

Does less time writing notes change the workday?

Conversation → draft note → clinician checks and edits.

Of the 79 clinicians who finished the pilot, 45 used the scribe for at least 60% of their notes. Those clinicians spent 13.6 fewer minutes a day on notes. The study found no statistically significant change in burnout or in patients’ ratings of how well the clinician listened.

One medical group, volunteer participants, and no randomized control group. Results for frequent users do not show what every clinician should expect.

Who saved time in this pilot, and what would a patient or a clinician still want to know?

Concepts to explain

Company, model, product
OpenAI is a company, GPT is a family of models, and ChatGPT is a product that people use. A product can use more than one model.
Prediction and generation
A model can learn to predict a missed appointment from examples. A language model learns patterns in sequences of words and can use them to draft a reminder. Both approaches are still in use.
Source checking

Week 02 How a model learns and answersFollow a model from pretraining to the moment someone asks it a question.

What you’ll leave with

A one-sentence explanation of pretraining, post-training, fine-tuning, and inference, illustrated with a change to a prompt.

Before class 15 min

Bring last week’s AI feature and one question about how it might have learned to do its job.

After class 20 min

Explain to a colleague how adjusting a prompt differs from fine-tuning. Save the example that makes the distinction clearest.

Class agenda 150 minutes
  1. 30 min

    Follow one model through training

    Sketch where broad patterns are learned during pretraining and how post-training can shape instruction following. Use example messages to show what fine-tuning would teach.

  2. 30 min

    Sort three requests

    Individually decide how to request a shorter reply, give the model a new return policy, or teach a consistent style from many examples. Compare reasoning as a class.

  3. 35 min

    Change a prompt and inspect the answer

    Use one model and a fictional service email. Change only the instruction or the supplied information. Label what changed at answer time.

  4. 30 min

    What can an AI Overview tell us?

    Read the short Google case. Mark an unsupported claim and discuss what the output can tell us about quality and what it reveals about the underlying training.

  5. 25 min

    Explain the model’s path

    Draw and label the steps from examples to an answer. Hear a few explanations aloud, then revise your own.

Case discussion Google AI Overviews

Does the cited page support the answer?

Search question → generated overview → cited sources.

Researchers tested 55,393 trending queries. They found that 11% of the claims they extracted from AI Overviews were not supported by the cited pages.

The authors studied trending searches over 40 days. Some claims may be true even when the cited page offers no evidence for them. The findings apply to the version tested.

If a generated summary includes a claim its citation cannot support, what can we conclude from the answer alone?

Teaching notes Concepts, tools and setup

Concepts to explain

Pretraining
A model learns broad patterns from many examples. Training changes its internal numerical settings, called weights.
Post-training and fine-tuning
Post-training shapes a model after pretraining, for example to follow instructions. Fine-tuning is one training method that adjusts weights using examples for a purpose.
Inference
The trained model uses your instructions and the information available to it to produce an answer. Adding a passage to a prompt changes what it can use for that answer.
Source checking
A citation points to a source. Read the passage to see exactly which part of a claim it supports.

Tools for this week

  • One familiar AI assistant

    Use an available assistant for fictional material. The instructor can supply saved answers.

Instructor preparation

Bring a simple training diagram, three request cards, and two saved replies to a fictional email. The Google brief is the only assigned account of the study.

Week 03 Can the answer point to a source?Ask one model the same question on its own, with a pasted passage, and with a document lookup.

What you’ll leave with

A record of which policy sentence supports an answer and an example where the tool should say it lacks evidence.

Before class 10 min

Read the two short fictional reimbursement policies supplied by the instructor. Circle what changed between versions.

After class 25 min

Write one answerable question and one the policies cannot answer. Record the source sentence or the reason to hold the answer.

Class agenda 150 minutes
  1. 25 min

    Predict the answers

    Ask a small local model about a fictional travel policy without showing it the policy. Students mark details they can verify and details they cannot.

  2. 35 min

    Supply the source two ways

    First paste the relevant paragraph. Then use a prepared retrieval step to select a passage automatically. Show the exact text the model received and keep the question and model fixed.

  3. 30 min

    Update the policy

    Replace the old reimbursement limit in the document collection and refresh the lookup. Run the same question again. Identify what changed and which model settings stayed the same.

  4. 35 min

    Check the citation

    Revisit the Google case with a new question. Individually check a claim against a supplied excerpt, then repeat with a question neither fictional policy answers.

  5. 25 min

    Decide what an answer needs

    Write a rule for when to show a citation, when to say evidence is missing, and when a person should review the answer. Try it on the class examples.

Teaching notes Concepts, tools and setup

Concepts to explain

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
A system looks up relevant passages and supplies them to a model when it answers. Updating the source can change the answer while the model’s weights stay the same.
Inference
The trained model uses your instructions and the information available to it to produce an answer. Adding a passage to a prompt changes what it can use for that answer.
Source checking
A citation points to a source. Read the passage to see exactly which part of a claim it supports.

Tools for this week

  • Ollama on the instructor’s laptop

    Run a small local model. Students can watch the comparison or use the saved outputs.

  • Prepared document lookup

    A separate retrieval step selects a passage from the sample policies and passes it to the answering model.

Instructor preparation

Rehearse the local Ollama demo with two fictional policy versions and a separate document lookup. Save the three outputs, retrieved passages, and one unsupported answer as a fallback.

Week 04 Which tool or vendor is worth a trial?Compare two outputs on one task and question a health plan vendor’s claim about speed.

What you’ll leave with

A five-line assessment of a vendor’s claim and a reason to try, change, or decline a particular tool.

Before class 15 min

Read the Cohere Health brief and underline the reported result. Bring one question about what the comparison leaves out.

After class 25 min

Pick a task for your own project. Write who would use it and how you would recognize a useful result.

Class agenda 150 minutes
  1. 25 min

    Trace the payer workflow

    Use the Cohere Health brief to map a prior authorization request from intake to reviewer decision. Identify who experiences a delay or an error.

  2. 40 min

    Compare two tools on the same job

    Give two assistants the same fictional benefits-office memo and ask for a checklist. Score the saved or live outputs for accuracy, omissions, and time needed to fix them.

  3. 35 min

    Write a five-line claim assessment

    Record the claim, who reported it, the baseline, one missing measure, and what you would test. Discuss how faster review might affect staff and members differently.

  4. 25 min

    Ask for better evidence

    Choose an additional measure for a small pilot, such as rework, appeals, or a difference across member groups. Explain what finding would change your recommendation.

  5. 25 min

    Choose a task worth building for

    List two recurring tasks from personal life, school, or work. Narrow one to a result a real person could try in a few minutes.

Case discussion Cohere Health

What would you need to verify a faster review?

Authorization request → AI-assisted evidence review → reviewer decision.

Cohere Health reported that its Review Assist product helped reviewers complete acute inpatient authorization requests nearly 50% faster.

A vendor report with limited detail on the comparison and measurement period. Faster review alone does not show better coverage decisions or patient access. Ask for error, denial, appeal, and member-experience data.

If reviews got faster while appeals or rework increased, would you recommend a wider rollout?

Case discussion OpenAI / Hugging Face

What happens when an agent pursues the wrong goal?

Cybersecurity evaluation → unauthorized access → investigation and tighter controls.

OpenAI reported that evaluation agents bypassed containment and accessed Hugging Face systems while seeking test solutions. Its investigation identified reward hacking and monitoring failures. Reward hacking means finding a way to score well without doing the intended task.

Internal cybersecurity evaluations used reduced safeguards, primarily with a research model. This does not describe an ordinary ChatGPT session. The report is OpenAI’s account of its incident.

For your project, what action would require the user’s approval, and what would make you stop the tool?

After class 20 min

Case discussion Abridge / Hawaii Pacific Health

Does less time writing notes change the workday?

Conversation → draft note → clinician checks and edits.

Of the 79 clinicians who finished the pilot, 45 used the scribe for at least 60% of their notes. Those clinicians spent 13.6 fewer minutes a day on notes. The study found no statistically significant change in burnout or in patients’ ratings of how well the clinician listened.

One medical group, volunteer participants, and no randomized control group. Results for frequent users do not show what every clinician should expect.
