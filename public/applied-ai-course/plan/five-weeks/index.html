Five-week plan

Try an everyday AI task, get an introduction to the core concepts, and evaluate a healthcare vendor. Start building in week 2 and hand over a usable tool in week 5.

5 in-person meetings · 12.5 hours of planned activities

What the shorter course leaves out

The first class introduces the concepts through short demonstrations and one everyday task. Students use one supported builder. The longer retrieval lab, two-tool comparison, Google case, and separate adoption lesson are reserved for the ten-week format. Both formats include a vendor scorecard and a usable final project.

Each three-hour meeting includes 150 minutes of planned activities and 30 minutes for breaks and questions. The original proposal was for 1.5 credits. The program would set credits for the chosen format.

What you’ll leave with

Before class 15 min

Fill out the opening survey. Include personal and work or school examples, familiar companies, and words you would like someone to explain.

After class 25 min

Choose a small personal, school, or work task. Bring one fictional example and check access to the class builder or editable starter.

Opening surveyEight questions about how students use AI and what they want to learn

Send 3–5 days before the first meeting. Ask for everyday examples without patient information, employer-confidential material, or private conversations. Summarize responses without names; access needs stay private.

  1. Where have you used AI in your personal life?

    List every use you can remember: the task, the tool if known, and whether it helped. Recipes, travel, shopping, writing, learning, images, and building things all count. ‘None yet’ and ‘not sure’ are valid answers.

  2. Where have you used AI at work or in your studies?

    Include tools you chose and features already inside another product: meeting summaries, email drafts, research, spreadsheets, coding, or a specific AI feature in an EHR such as Epic. Name the feature if you know it; using an EHR does not necessarily mean using AI.

  3. Which companies and products do you recognize?

    For each, select: not familiar / heard of it / tried it / use regularly. Include OpenAI (ChatGPT, Codex), Anthropic (Claude), Google (Gemini), Microsoft (Copilot), Epic, Abridge, Cohere Health, IQVIA, Ollama, and Lovable, plus an open ‘something else’ field.

  4. Which AI capabilities have you encountered?

    Select any: summarize, draft, search, transcribe, generate images or audio, predict, analyze data, write code, automate steps, or take actions in another app. Add an example or select ‘not sure.’

  5. Which terms have you heard, and which could you explain?

    For AI, machine learning, generative AI, LLM, training, post-training, fine-tuning, inference, prompt, token, context window, hallucination, RAG, agent, automation, API, and vibe coding, select: new to me / heard it / could explain it in one sentence. Add any other buzzwords you want unpacked.

  6. How confident do you feel today?

    Rate 1–5, from not yet confident to very confident: using AI personally; using it at work or school; explaining how it works; questioning a product claim; and building a tool with AI. Repeat these questions at the end of the course.

  7. What would you like to do or understand with AI?

    Share one question and one task you would like help with. You might want a starting point, uses beyond ChatGPT, a way to evaluate vendors, clearer explanations, or help building something. Include a useful or disappointing experience if you have one. A project idea can come later.

  8. What would help you participate?

    Share any device, account, access, or accessibility needs privately with the instructor. This helps the instructor prepare. You do not need to buy anything for the survey.

Case discussion Abridge / Hawaii Pacific Health

Does less time writing notes change the workday?

Conversation → draft note → clinician checks and edits.

Of the 79 clinicians who finished the pilot, 45 used the scribe for at least 60% of their notes. Those clinicians spent 13.6 fewer minutes a day on notes. The study found no statistically significant change in burnout or in patients’ ratings of how well the clinician listened.

One medical group, volunteer participants, and no randomized control group. Results for frequent users do not show what every clinician should expect.

If a health system said this tool gave every clinician time back, which part of the pilot would you ask to see?

Teaching notes Concepts, tools and setup

Concepts to explain

AI, machine learning, and generative AI
AI includes systems that predict, generate, or act. Machine learning learns patterns from data. A large language model (LLM) uses learned patterns to work with language and is one kind of generative AI.
Company, model, product
OpenAI is a company, GPT is a family of models, and ChatGPT is a product that people use. A product can use more than one model.
Pretraining
A model learns broad patterns from many examples. Training changes its internal numerical settings, called weights.
Post-training and fine-tuning
Post-training shapes a model after pretraining, for example to follow instructions. Fine-tuning is one training method that adjusts weights using examples for a purpose.
Inference
The trained model uses your instructions and the information available to it to produce an answer. Adding a passage to a prompt changes what it can use for that answer.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
A system looks up relevant passages and supplies them to a model when it answers. Updating the source can change the answer while the model’s weights stay the same.
Prompting
Tell the tool what you want to do, supply relevant information, and describe a useful result. Inspect its answer and revise one part of the request at a time.
Choosing what to share
Use fictional or public material for class exercises. At work, check which tools and types of information your organization permits. Review an output before sharing it or acting on it.

Tools for this week

  • AI directory

    Look up companies, models, and products. Match tasks to chat assistants, document tools, meeting or spreadsheet features, and AI coding tools. Start with examples from the survey.

  • One familiar AI assistant

    Use an available assistant for fictional material. The instructor can supply saved answers.

  • Ollama on the instructor’s laptop

    Run a small local model. Students can watch the comparison or use the saved outputs.

Instructor preparation

Case discussion Cohere Health

What would you need to verify a faster review?

Authorization request → AI-assisted evidence review → reviewer decision.

Cohere Health reported that its Review Assist product helped reviewers complete acute inpatient authorization requests nearly 50% faster.

A vendor report with limited detail on the comparison and measurement period. Faster review alone does not show better coverage decisions or patient access. Ask for error, denial, appeal, and member-experience data.

What would a reviewer, a member, and a health plan each want to know besides the time saved?

Teaching notes Concepts, tools and setup

Concepts to explain

Baseline and measure
The baseline is what happened before, or in a comparison group. The measure tells you what someone counted, for whom, and over what period.
Vendor scorecard
Compare the task and intended user, evidence against a baseline, data access and retention, workflow and human review, and total cost. Mark what the available evidence answers and what you still need to ask or test.
Build brief
Write down who will use the tool, the one task they should be able to finish, and what a good result looks like.
Vibe coding
Describe what you want to a coding assistant, try what it makes, and ask for a specific change. You are responsible for checking the result.

Tools for this week

    Use an available assistant for fictional material. The instructor can supply saved answers.

  • Healthcare vendor directory

    Find a product and its intended user. Use the vendor scorecard to record evidence, data questions, workflow fit, and costs to investigate.

  • Claude Artifacts or the class starter

    Use the instructor-supported builder or an editable starter that opens in a browser. Keep using the same one throughout the project.

Instructor preparation

Week 03 Finish one task from start to finishAutomate a useful step, set the limits on what your tool may do, and try two examples.

What you’ll leave with

A working project, two recorded trials, and a decision about what needs human approval.

Before class 10 min

Reopen your project and bring two fictional examples with the outcomes you expect.

After class 25 min

Save the working version and draft two sentences telling someone how to try it. Bring one example that gives the tool trouble.

Class agenda 150 minutes
  1. 25 min

    When should a tool stop?

    Discuss the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident. From the brief, identify a permission, an approval step, and a reason to stop. Choose one boundary for your own project.

  2. 20 min

    Trace your task

    Sketch the steps from the first click to the result. Identify a step the tool could do for you, such as sorting notes, calculating a result, or preparing a checklist. Mark where a person should review an output or could recover from a mistake.

  3. 65 min

    Build the whole path

    Use the same builder to make that step work with a supplied input and a visible result. Describe one change at a time, check what the builder made, and keep a working version.

  4. 40 min

    Run two trials

    Try the expected example and one variation. Record what happened, fix the most important issue you see, and repeat the trial.

Case discussion OpenAI / Hugging Face

What happens when an agent pursues the wrong goal?

Cybersecurity evaluation → unauthorized access → investigation and tighter controls.

OpenAI reported that evaluation agents bypassed containment and accessed Hugging Face systems while seeking test solutions. Its investigation identified reward hacking and monitoring failures. Reward hacking means finding a way to score well without doing the intended task.

Internal cybersecurity evaluations used reduced safeguards, primarily with a research model. This does not describe an ordinary ChatGPT session. The report is OpenAI’s account of its incident.

Who should be able to authorize the next step, and how would they know when to stop?

Teaching notes Concepts, tools and setup

Concepts to explain

Permissions and oversight
Decide what a tool may read or change, what needs a person’s approval, and when it must stop.
Workflow, automation, and agent
A workflow connects steps from input to result. Automation runs some of those steps for you. An agent can choose its next step or tool within the permissions it has.
Test case
Record an input, what should happen, what actually happened, and whether the tool passed.

Week 04 Test, fix, and share progressLearn from a pharmacy handoff, run three tests, and watch someone try a project.

What you’ll leave with

Three test results, one fix or clearly stated limit, and instructions improved by watching a first-time user.

Before class 15 min

Bring your working project, draft instructions, and three inputs: ordinary, missing a detail, and unexpected.

After class 30 min

Finish one change suggested by the demos, rerun the affected test, and check that another person can open your link or file.

Class agenda 150 minutes
  1. 15 min

    Catch a missing detail

    Use the SSM Health medication-access brief. Walk through a fictional request missing a required field and decide what staff should check before it moves forward.

  2. 40 min

    Test and fix your own tool

    Individually record expected and actual results for three inputs. Fix one issue and rerun that test. If it persists, explain the limit where the user will see it.

  3. 20 min

    Prepare a handoff

    Write how to open the tool, an example to use, and what a good result looks like. Test your link or file from a fresh window.

  4. 75 min

    Progress demos and time to revise

    Invite up to five volunteers to give a first-time user control of their tool for six minutes. Discuss where the user hesitated. Everyone uses the remaining time to change one thing in their own project and try it again.

Case discussion SSM Health / medication access

Can AI help a medication request move forward?

Chart information → prepared authorization request → staff validation and submission.

SSM Health’s contributor reports 30–40% shorter medication prior authorization turnaround. AI supported information gathering and preparation while staff handled validation and exceptions.

The summary gives no sample size, detailed comparison, or measurement dates. Integration gaps, output checking, and staff trust remained challenges. This is not an independent trial.

Which missing detail would matter to the person waiting for their medication, and who should catch it?

Link to week 5
The project and what students hand in

Choose one task that would help someone at home, at school, or at work. Your tool could organize notes, plan meals, or automate a step in a recurring task. Build it with an AI tool you can access. Use fictional or public material for classroom examples. A project built with AI can work without an AI connection.

Give another person a way to open the tool and an example to try. Record the expected and actual results for an ordinary input, a missing detail, and an unexpected input. Show one limitation and explain one AI idea in your own words. The final reflection draws on the same work.
