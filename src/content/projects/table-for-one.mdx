## Why I built it

On Saturdays, my son and I visit a nearby children's museum where we have a membership. Afterward, we get two cheeseburgers from STK. It's a small routine I wanted to make easier.

Each order meant another call to the restaurant's voice AI and another round of the same questions. Explaining two cheeseburgers took too long. I wanted to tap our usual order or say what we wanted, review it once, and let my own assistant handle the call.

That became Table for One, a private web app for my takeout orders. I started with our STK order, then expanded it to saved restaurants and different meals. The new build is ready for further testing; restaurant calling remains disabled.

## The experience

I can speak or type an order, or load a saved favorite. The app turns the request into editable fields for the exact restaurant location, each item and quantity, special requests, and pickup time. Missing or ambiguous details need an answer before I can continue.

I choose a card nickname or payment at pickup, add a tip, and set a maximum total. A separate review screen shows the whole order before submission. Speaking or filling in a draft cannot start a call. During a call, the restaurant must confirm the items, price, and pickup time within those limits.

If a modification is refused or the conversation needs my help, the assistant can ask me to join the same call. The app tracks the confirmed total, ready time, and outcome. Payment at pickup stays marked as due, and an unclear outcome stays unresolved.

I chose AWS because I wanted to become more familiar with its tools by building something I'd use myself. It gave me a reason to learn how hosting, storage, permissions, and background work fit together.

S3 and CloudFront serve the website. API Gateway and Lambda handle requests, and DynamoDB saves orders and preferences. EC2 runs the ongoing audio connection only when a call needs it. Secrets Manager holds provider credentials, while CloudWatch and SNS support operational logs and email alerts. With only a few calls a month, I can stop the voice worker between orders, though storage and other services still have costs.

## Decisions and design lessons

**A favorite still needs review.** Saved items remove repetition, but the restaurant, quantities, pickup time, and spending limit remain visible. The assistant cannot quietly swap an item or accept a higher total.

**An unclear outcome needs checking.** A dropped connection does not prove that an order failed. The app preserves the submitted request for recovery and gives me a way to take over the conversation.

**Payment needs its own boundary.** Twilio provides the phone connection; the restaurant handles payment. Saved card labels contain the nickname, brand, and last four digits, without full card details. Real card payment needs my private participation, and automatic card entry is not implemented. An order accepted for payment at pickup still has money due.

## Current state and next step

As of October 9, 2026, the v8 implementation passes 379 automated tests and 19 local simulated-call scenarios. It supports saved restaurants and favorites, voice or text drafts, multiple items, requested pickup times, review, and takeover controls. These checks use synthetic data and do not prove real restaurant or iPhone behavior.

The older STK v7 release is deployed on AWS with calling disabled. The new v8 build has not been deployed or evaluated in new real-model or audio calls. Next are model-conversation evaluation, a controlled call with a consenting test recipient, and physical iPhone dictation checks before enabling restaurant calls. Real-card automation remains outside this release.
